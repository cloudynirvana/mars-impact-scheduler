Every twenty six months, Earth catches up with Mars, and a door opens for a few weeks.
I searched every departure date and every flight time between twenty twenty eight and twenty thirty seven, using NASA's own planetary positions.
Six thousand seven hundred and seventy workable trajectories. And they fall into exactly four windows. Miss one, and you wait two years.
Twenty thirty three is the cheapest to leave on. But twenty thirty five is the gentlest to arrive on. It costs nearly twice the launch energy, and the payload hits the atmosphere more than one and a half kilometres per second slower. A far easier arrival to survive.
Now the part that surprised me. Once you have picked your trajectory, the direction you arrive from is locked in. But Mars is still turning, at fourteen point six degrees an hour.
So shift your arrival by a few hours, and the same trajectory lands you on a different continent. The longitude is free. You just choose the minute you arrive.
Latitude is what you pay for. You buy it by aiming off centre, and letting the planet's gravity bend you round.
Which leads to the awkward result. Hellas is the deepest basin on Mars, six kilometres down, with nearly twice the air to brake against. It is the best place to arrive. And it is the most expensive target in all four windows, because it sits far into the south, while every one of these approaches comes in near the equator.
One thing this does not decide is what arrives. Martian soil carries perchlorate salts. Toxic to us, and to anything we would want to grow. What I modelled is a soil conditioning package aimed at them, not a living one. Deliberately delivering organisms to Mars is barred under the Outer Space Treaty. The trajectory mathematics is identical either way.
The trajectory wants the equator. The entry physics wants Hellas. Somebody has to choose.
